Mathematical magic trick lets you work out the weekday of any date

A date-calculation magic trick: use a little mathematics—and a little help from Lewis Carroll—to find the weekday of any date.

Words by Mathematical Life January 4, 2025 4-minute read

Working out the day of the week is a delightful mathematical trick. With a little modular arithmetic, the numbers on a calendar become a mental-math game. Lewis Carroll—the author of Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland—was fascinated by the relationship between dates and weekdays.

The trick is to break a date into a few small numbers: start with the year, add a leap-year adjustment, choose a “key” for the month, and then add the day. At each step, keep only the remainder. The answer falls between 0 and 6, corresponding to the seven days of the week.

Month keys can be memorized through a repeating pattern. Leap years require special care in January and February. What looks like a secret code is simply a neat application of arithmetic modulo 7 to the calendar.

Turn the calendar into mental math

In 1887, Lewis Carroll published a method for quickly finding the weekday of a date. The idea is not to memorize an entire calendar, but to assign a small value to the year, month, and day, then take the total modulo 7.

The method also reminds us that dates are not isolated numbers. Every seven days, the weekday cycle starts again; an extra day in a leap year shifts later dates forward by one day.

“Break a big problem into small remainders, and the calendar can turn in your head.”

The idea behind the trick

The principle: compress elapsed days into a remainder

Weekdays repeat every 7 days. So instead of memorizing every date, count the days since a reference date and take the remainder after dividing by 7.

Count forward from January 1, year 1

In the Gregorian calendar, this reference date is a Monday. For a target date in year y, month m, and day d, the number of days in all completed years is:

Y = 365(y − 1) + ⌊(y − 1)/4⌋ − ⌊(y − 1)/100⌋ + ⌊(y − 1)/400⌋

Instead of counting every day before the month, use its month code C from the table—the number of days before that month, reduced modulo 7. Add the leap-year adjustment L (1 in a leap year after February, otherwise 0) and the day of the month:

Weekday index = (Y + C + L + d) mod 7

Here, Sunday is 0, Monday is 1, and so on. The formula includes a +1 offset because January 1, year 1 is a Monday, and uses d rather than d − 1.

Example: January 4, 2025

For the 2,024 completed years, the leap-year correction adds a day every 4 years, subtracts one every 100 years, then adds one back every 400 years:

Y = 365×2024 + 506 − 20 + 5
= 739251

There are no completed months before January, and 2025 is not a leap year:

N = 739251 + 0 + 0 + (4−1)
= 739254 (739254 + 1) mod 7 = 6
0 Sun · 1 Mon · 2 Tue · 3 Wed
4 Thu · 5 Fri · 6 Sat

So the answer is Saturday. A year is a leap year if it is divisible by 4, except century years must also be divisible by 400.

Note: This cumulative-day derivation is included to explain the modulo 7 idea. It is not a verbatim account of Carroll’s original mental arithmetic method.
